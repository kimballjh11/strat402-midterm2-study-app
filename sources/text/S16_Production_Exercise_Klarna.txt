## SHEET: Overview (6 rows x 10 cols)
A1: "Production Exercise: Klarna's AI Assistant and Human Agents"
A2: 'Overview\nIn late January 2024, Klarna, the buy-now-pay-later company, launched an AI customer service assistant built on OpenAI\'s models. A month later Klarna announced that the assistant had already handled 2.3 million conversations, two-thirds of all customer service chats, and was doing the equivalent work of 700 full-time agents. Customers resolved their errands in under 2 minutes, compared with 11 minutes before. Klarna froze hiring and let its human customer service staff shrink through attrition. By late 2025, Klarna said the assistant was doing the work of 853 agents.\n\nIn May 2025, the CEO changed course. He said cost had been "a too predominant evaluation factor" and had led to lower quality, and Klarna began recruiting human agents again. AI and humans are not perfect substitutes: the assistant handles routine errands well, but disputes, fraud, and unusual cases still need a person, and a conversation the AI handles badly comes back as a repeat contact.\n\nIn this exercise you will estimate Klarna\'s customer service production function, check whether Klarna is using the cost-minimizing mix of AI assistant hours and human agent hours, find that mix, and quantify what moving to it would save. Output (Q) is customer conversations resolved per month. Capital (K) is AI assistant hours: AI-handled conversations × 2 minutes. Labor (L) is human agent hours. The monthly data on the Data tab are simulated for this exercise, calibrated to Klarna\'s public statements; they are not Klarna\'s internal records.\n\nInstructions: Complete Steps 1–9 in the Exercise tab using production data from the Data tab. Enter your answers in the yellow cells, and use formulas that reference your regression output and the data rather than typed-in numbers. Then answer the questions at the bottom.'

## SHEET: Log-linear Regression (6 rows x 1 cols)
A1: 'Steps to Perform Log-Linear Regression'
A2: "A Cobb-Douglas production function has the form Q = A·K^a·L^b, where A is a constant and a and b are parameters that define how inputs are turned into output. Because a and b are exponents, you can't estimate them with an ordinary linear regression on Q, K, and L. Cobb-Douglas functions have diminishing marginal rates of technical substitution: as one input replaces more and more of the other, it takes more and more of it to keep output the same. That makes it a good fit for AI and human agents, which can substitute for each other but not perfectly.\n\n1. Take the natural log of both sides: ln(Q) = ln(A) + a·ln(K) + b·ln(L). This is a standard linear form in which ln(Q) is the dependent variable, ln(A) is the intercept, and a and b are the coefficients on ln(K) and ln(L). On the Data tab, fill in the ln(Q), ln(K), and ln(L) columns with the LN function and copy the formulas down for all months.\n\n2. Go to Data → Data Analysis (you may need to enable the Analysis ToolPak add-in) and choose Regression. Set the Input Y Range to the ln(Q) column, and select both the ln(K) and ln(L) columns for the Input X Range. Include the column titles in both ranges and check the Labels box so the output labels the variables correctly.\n\n3. Under Output options, choose Output Range and select the cell indicated in Step 1 on the Exercise tab. Click OK.\n\n4. Interpret the results. The coefficients on ln(K) and ln(L) are a and b. To convert the estimated function back to Cobb-Douglas form, exponentiate both sides, which undoes the log. Don't forget to exponentiate the intercept as well: A = e^(intercept)."
## SHEET: Using Solver to Cost Minimize (23 rows x 1 cols)
A1: 'Using Solver to Cost Minimize in Production Theory'
A2: "Overview\n\nTo find the cost-minimizing input mix, you need the point where the slope of the isoquant (MPL/MPK) equals the slope of the isocost line (w/r). In other words, the ratio of the marginal products must equal the ratio of the input prices.\n\nOne approach is to fix K or L and vary the other until MPL/MPK = w/r. That finds the right mix, but almost certainly not on the isoquant for your output goal: you can't cut one input without adding the other and still produce the same output.\n\nEconomists solve this as a constrained optimization (with a Lagrange multiplier): minimize total cost subject to producing a target level of output. Because the production function already links K and L, the slopes will automatically be equal at the minimum-cost point. Instead of the calculus, you'll use Solver to do the same thing.\n\nThe total cost function is C = wL + rK, the input quantities times their prices. This is Solver's objective. The constraint is that Q, from your estimated production function, must equal the average monthly output.\n\nSteps to Using Solver\n\n1. In Step 6 on the Exercise tab, L, K, w, r, Cost, and Q are listed in separate cells.\n\nL=_____\nK=_____\nw=_____\nr=_____\nC=_____\nQ=_____\n\n2. For L and K, enter starting values (the monthly averages work well); Solver will change them. For w and r, use the given prices. Enter formulas for Cost and Q that reference those cells.\n\n3. Open Solver (Data → Solver; you may need to enable the Solver add-in).\n\n4. Set Objective: the Cost cell. Select Min.\n\n5. By Changing Variable Cells: the L and K cells, separated by a comma.\n\n6. Subject to the Constraints: click Add, select the Q cell, choose =, and reference the Q target cell (the average monthly output). Click OK.\n\n7. Click Solve. Solver will find the cost-minimizing L and K. If Solver doesn't land on the target output, go to Options and set Constraint Precision to 0.000001, or use Automatic Scaling. Then check that MPL/MPK equals w/r."
