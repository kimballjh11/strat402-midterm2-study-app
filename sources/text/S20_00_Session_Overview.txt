 Game Theory Exercise 

 Background 

 In October 2019, Twitter announced that it would stop running political ads. Facebook kept running them. Both companies were under pressure from activists and regulators over misleading content on their platforms, and both faced the same choice. They could ban political ads and give up that revenue, or keep them and risk government regulation. What each company stood to gain or lose depended on what the other one did. 

 In this assignment you will go back to 2019 and model that choice as a game. Using each company's 2019 ad revenue from its 10-K, you will build the payoffs, find the Nash equilibrium, and check whether either company has a dominant strategy. Then you will change the game twice, first by letting Twitter move first and then by changing the regulatory risk, and see when the order of moves changes the outcome.

 Instructions 

 The workbook has three tabs, one for each part. Read the scenario at the top of each tab before you start, because each part changes the game. Links to both companies' 2019 10-Ks are on the Part 1 tab. 

 Part 1 (steps 1–6). Find each company's 2019 ad revenue, build the payoff matrix for a one-shot simultaneous game, and explain each payoff. Then find the Nash equilibrium and decide whether either company has a dominant strategy. 

 Part 2 (steps 1–4). Draw the same game as a sequential game with Twitter moving first, solve it with backward induction, and compare the result to Part 1. 

 Part 3 (steps 1–5). Use the new regulatory risk assumptions to build both the payoff matrix and the game tree, then compare your results across all three parts. 

 Report payoffs in millions of dollars, with losses as negative numbers. In the payoff matrices, list payoffs as (Facebook, Twitter). In the game trees, list them as (Twitter, Facebook), since Twitter moves first.
