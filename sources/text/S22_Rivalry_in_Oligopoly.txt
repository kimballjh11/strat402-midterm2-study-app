Rivalry in Oligopoly
Situated on a continuum between markets of one firm (monopoly) and markets of many firms
(perfect competition) are markets of a few firms. These markets are called oligopoly. In
oligopolies the number of firms ranges from two (duopoly) up to some number, maybe 8-12,
perhaps as many as 15. Stating the upper bound precisely is difficult. When there are enough
firms that each becomes a price taker, the industry is no longer an oligopoly. The key
determinant is whether the firms are acting and reacting to one another in repeated waves of
competition. If a single firm’s actions have enough impact on the market demand curve to affect
the market price-quantity combination and thus prospects for other firms, the firms’ actions are
interdependent and usually oligopoly is present.

In oligopoly, each firm maximizes profits by considering its rivals’ reactions to its own strategic
moves. For example, before a firm sets a particular price on a product, establishes a particular
production schedule, develops new product features, offers new programs, or makes other
competitive moves, it considers how its rivals are likely to respond to its actions. For example, in
a duopoly, Firm 1 reasons as follows: If I do a, then Firm 2 will do b. But if Firm 2 does b, then I
must do c. But if I do c, then they will do d. But if they do d, I will get the best of them by doing e.
But if I do e, I know for sure that they will probably do f, and so I will do g. But if I do g, they will
… and so it goes. Where does it end? It ends at a point when the firms reach a decision that they
no longer desire to change because it’s the best possible decision to all possible rival actions. In
game theory, this point is also called a Nash equilibrium: Each firm is playing its optimal
response to other firms, and there is no incentive for them to deviate from that response. Thus,
the reasoning of game theory is very useful in predicting the outcomes of oligopolistic rivalry.

In practice, firms work the steps through reason before they play them in the market. Since they
already conjecture what their competitor will do in response to their move, and what they will do
in response to their competitor’s move and so forth, they simply reason through the game before
they begin, find the equilibrium strategy, and play it. Each rival knows that the others are playing
the game in the same way, so each just plays the equilibrium strategy.

The equilibrium strategy isn’t always exactly clear. Firms may not know the exact form of the
demand curve, for example, or exactly how much the extent of their differentiation matters to
consumers. In these cases, a series of actions may be taken by the firm to work out an optimal
strategy while it observes its competitors’ responses. But such experimentation, surprisingly,
seems to be the exception rather than the rule. In real markets, even complex ones, equilibria are
reached rapidly, often with little adjustment, i.e. no experimentation. Firms simply arrive
together at the equilibrium strategy as they make conjectures about what their rivals will do.
Such conjectures in some ways become self-fulfilling with respect to overall market demand
realized, but the gaming aspect of the conjectures assures that each firm takes their optimal share
of whatever pie is created.

The classic case of the foregoing is the corn wet milling industry. Throughout the 1960s, a stable
oligopoly of corn processors prevailed in an industry that produced corn starch and corn syrup.
But in 1972, a new technology for creating high-fructose corn syrup appeared. Eleven major
competitors had to decide how much new plant capacity to build, knowing that other rivals were
also building capacity. If too much industry capacity were collectively created, prices would be
too low to make a profit for any producer in the industry. On the other hand, if any one
producer built too little capacity, others building more would take higher share and profits. Thus,
they all reasoned simultaneously and without coordination on how much capacity to produce,
each estimating the size of the market while taking into account expected rival actions.

Interestingly, they achieved a stable equilibrium outcome in which the amount supplied to the

The tendency by oligopolists to reason through to the equilibrium outcome before taking
strategic actions also results in what might be termed “tacit” collusion, or truce. If the firm
reasons that undercutting the competition will bring it certain market share, the firm must realize
that it is not the only firm thinking the same way. Knowing that its rivals will undercut its price
in turn, and that the firm must then further cut price to retain share leads the firm to the obvious
conclusion that nobody wins if the firm cuts price. So the firm refuses to do so. Its rivals reason
the same way, and even without explicit coordination, they reach a collusive outcome, a pricing
truce. Furthermore, the firm may realize that if it raises its price, rivals will view the opportunity
as one to be exploited either by keeping price low and taking share, or by raising price and
earning profits. If the firm thinks they prefer the latter (and if firm demand curves support the
reasoning), the firm will expect the rival to respond to price increases with increases of its own.
On the other hand, if the firm thinks the rival prefers the former—to leave price low to take share
when the firm raises its price—then the firm may never raise its price in the first place because it
knows it will lose share. But here’s the catch: the rival knows that the firm knows this, so the rival
will interpret a price increase as a collusive signal. And the firm knows that the rival knows that
the firm knows that the rival knows (…etc.) how to interpret a price increase, so the firm knows
that the rival will also raise price. Thus, when a rival raises price, it may signal a new collusive
move that others simply match.2 This response behavior is explored more rigorously in a later
section.

The full-fare airline industry illustrates well the tendency of oligopolists to reach tacitly collusive
pricing outcomes. Price hikes are gladly matched by rivals, while price cuts are likewise
aggressively matched. For this reason, oligopolists play the game carefully. Price hikes are
rarely discussed with much fanfare, yet ubiquitous information means that competitors can
immediately respond with hikes of their own. In contrast, price cuts among the full-fare airlines
are well defined and publicized: Full-fare carriers cutting fares are very specific about the dates,
routes, prices, and other constraints on fare sales. This information is immediately seen by rivals
who respond in kind within the defined parameters of the first mover. All tacitly agree that when
the fare sale expires, it’s back to business as usual. No firm is so foolish as to cut all fares all the
time for an indeterminate length of time. Not until January 2005, that is, when Delta did exactly
that. In that case, however, Delta’s strategy was clearly to abandon the oligopolistic norms of full-
fare carriers in order to compete more favorably with discount fare carriers like JetBlue and
Southwest. Even an oligopolist’s neat little world can be shaken by a disruptive innovator.

Tacitly collusive outcomes arise in oligopoly partly due to the repetitive nature of the
competition. Oligopolists engage each period with no certain ending date. As a result, they have
incentive to avoid certain types of aggressive competition in their own best interests. The
incentive to avoid competition may lead them to test high price strategies for example as a way to
offer a sort of truce on price competition. If their rivals follow suit, a norm is established, at least
temporarily.

Once a norm is established in an industry, oligopolists tend to monitor one another carefully.
Violations of norms can be swiftly punished if rivals have the capacity to do so. Punishment
might entail periods of price cutting or other types of gaming by rivals that demonstrate they will
not tolerate spoilers. If monitoring is difficult, or retaliation weak, defections from norms become
commonplace and industry profitability suffers. Monitoring is especially difficult and retaliation
especially half-hearted as the number of firms increases. Such industries rapidly move toward
perfect competition, in part because of numbers but also because of strategic behavior.
Unfortunately for the oligopolists, as in airlines, spoilers often enter an industry with a disruptive
model and care little about established norms. Amazon.com is a good example in this regard as
it clearly entered bookselling with prices that oligopolists Borders and Barnes & Noble simply
would never have considered on their own. Established oligopolists are often incapable of
responding to the disruptor because their cost structures or value chains simply won’t support an
effective response, hence the power of disruptive innovations.

To be clear, oligopolists reason in response to rivals with their own best interests at heart. In that
regard they are profit maximizing. But even when they are not tacitly colluding to grow profits,
they are still keying off of the behavior of rivals as they make strategic choices. This by itself is a
form of collusion, but it is a completely legitimate form. Thus, while regulators are concerned
about collusion among oligopolists, such collusion is difficult to detect and nearly impossible to
regulate.

Oligopolists operate precisely the same as firms in other market settings in one important feature:
They are profit maximizing. An oligopolist computes its marginal revenue (MR) from the demand
curve and sets MR equal to marginal cost (MC) to find price or quantity combinations that are
optimal, or profit maximizing. But here is the point of departure. The price or quantity
combination that is optimal is always dependent on the price or quantity combination selected by
rivals (since the moves of each firm affect the market equilibrium on the demand curve). Thus,
each firm has a range of possible prices or quantities that it can offer dependent on the price or
quantity choices of rivals. In game theory, such schedules of responses are called strategies. A
strategy is a set of actions for every possible rival move. For oligopolists, another name for the
complete set of responses is a “reaction function.” More on reaction functions below.

                              Types of Competition in Oligopoly

This note has been rather general in referring to oligopoly, conveniently treating all oligopolies
the same. In reality, there are differences, based largely on the strategic variables that are used in
competition. Strategic variables are the levers used by oligopolists to compete. They include such
mechanisms as pricing, capacity decisions (quantity), lead time, or other non-price mechanisms
(e.g. frequent flier programs, coupons, geographic location choices, etc.). Likewise, oligopolists
may produce homogeneous goods, or goods that are at least moderately differentiated. 3

Despite the variety of oligopolies suggested by the list of strategic variables and differentiation
considerations, only four archetypical or pure-type oligopolies are commonly modeled to
describe real-world competitive dynamics. For homogeneous-good oligopolies, competition in
quantities is called Cournot, competition in prices is called Bertrand (or here Standard Bertrand),

Cournot

In a Cournot oligopoly, homogeneous-good firms compete primarily by setting output quantity
and letting the price float on the demand curve. The use of quantity as the main strategic
variable occurs primarily in commodity goods markets such as oil or agriculture when these
markets are not otherwise perfectly competitive. Although many agricultural markets are
traditionally considered perfectly competitive, the formation of large cooperatives essentially
creates a Cournot oligopoly overlay in which the amount of produce made available for sale by
each of the large cooperatives is established through Cournot-style gaming. The corn wet milling
market mentioned above is another example.

The world’s largest oil producers function in a type of Cournot oligopoly via 11 country
organizations that are part of the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC). OPEC,
a cartel, sets output targets for each of its members. Since OPEC explicitly recognizes that its
member nations are developing countries with significant economic development needs, a
primary objective is to create “stability” (read profitability) for its members. In fact, OPEC has
the stated objective of “ensur[ing] a balance between supply and demand” in world oil markets. 5
However, since price regulates this balance, what they’re really saying is that they control output
to achieve a target price. Thus, OPEC practices a very explicit form of Cournot collusion.

Cournot oligopolists set quantity output where marginal revenue (MR) equals marginal cost
(MC). Each firm derives marginal revenue by multiplying (inverse) demand by its own quantity

The Cournot-Nash equilibrium is the expected outcome for oligopolists that profit-maximize by
taking their rival’s output as given. If these Cournot oligopolists were explicitly colluding,
however, they might be able to achieve higher profits. This is the goal of a cartel. Recall that the
monopoly level of output is the profit maximizing output for a single firm and that it is the point
at which any particular market maximizes total profit. So if the oligopolists collude to simply
split the monopoly output, both of them would receive monopoly profits. This collusive outcome
is shown in Figure 2 on line m. Notice that the line intersects at the monopoly output levels of
each individual firm. Thus, the line represents all possible combinations of output that the firms
could respectively produce to generate monopoly profits. A natural expectation is that the firms
will split the monopoly quantity evenly at point m and each firm will produce the collusive
quantity qc.

Collusive agreements of this sort, however, are notoriously difficult to hold, as evidenced by the
oil cartel. Once output quotas have been set by OPEC, it seems a number of member countries
produce more than their quota. To see why, imagine that OPEC has only two members, Firm 1
and Firm 2. They hold a meeting and decide to limit each firm to the collusive output, qc (point m).
But if Firm 1 knows that Firm 2 will produce qc, then it maximizes its profits by producing on its
reaction function at a point horizontal to qc on R1. This lies somewhere below point d on the
graphic and represents a quantity output level above even the Cournot-Nash equilibrium. If Firm
2 thinks Firm 1 will defect from the collusive outcome and produce on its reaction function, it
maximizes profit by producing on its own reaction function, and the duopolists are right back
into the Cournot game. This incentive to defect from collusive agreements means that they often
don’t last long.

Bertrand

Standard Bertrand oligopoly is competition in price among homogeneous producers. These are
markets in which there are few information constraints so that consumers can see most prices
most of the time (in modeling, it’s all prices all of the time) and goods are truly homogeneous.
Markets fitting these constraints were at one time rare—non-consolidated commodity markets
were among the few good examples. However, in an Internet world, Bertrand markets are much
more common. Indeed, the Internet has made many markets commodity-like. The Internet
lowers constraints to the free flow of information so that sellers are required to compete
vigorously on price with other sellers of the same good. With few information constraints price
is king—the lowest price impresses consumers and they buy, rewarding low-price firms with
business. Naturally, this fact creates intense price competition among rivals.

In the formal model, we assume two firms with homogeneous technology offer homogeneous
products to a market. Consumers see all prices, there are no switching costs, no transaction costs,

Suppose Firm 1 sets price at P. Firm 2 has a choice to set its price either above, equal to, or below
Firm 1’s price. If it sets price above, Firm 2 sells 0, since consumers prefer the lowest price and
give all their business to Firm 1. If Firm 2 sets price equal to Firm 1, each firm obtains half of the
market. If Firm 2 sets price below Firm 1, then Firm 2 takes the whole market since it is offering
the lowest price. Since by setting price just slightly below price P, Firm 1 can take the whole
market rather than just fifty percent, Firm 2 always prefers undercutting in the short run. But
Firm 1 knows this, so it does not hold its price at P either. Anticipating that it will be undercut by
Firm 2, Firm 1 has already determined to lower price P to be sure it falls below whatever Firm 2
establishes as their price. But Firm 2 knows that Firm 1 is thinking this way and has already
anticipated that reaction, and so has gone even further below Firm 1. But Firm 1 knows that Firm
2 knows that Firm 1 knows this, so it anticipates that particular price cut already. Proceeding in
this fashion the firms “race to the bottom,” stopping only when they arrive at marginal cost.

The reality is that the above reasoning plays out instantaneously before prices are set in the first
place. Thus, the standard Bertrand-Nash equilibrium is P1=P2=MC1=MC2. Prices are simply set
at marginal cost, which here we assumed were equal between the two firms.

A question arises about whether these standard Bertrand oligopolists can sustain collusion at a
point above marginal cost. Must they race to the bottom? The answer is no, not necessarily, but
avoidance of this is extremely difficult in these markets. Knowing that they will play the game
together every period for some unknown time horizon could give them strong incentive to
cooperate. In this context, cooperation means that Firm 2 matches price P rather than
undercutting. Observing this, Firm 1 holds steady and the firms split the market. This collusive
outcome is sustainable only when it clearly benefits the firms over the long run. Unfortunately,
the pull of taking the entire market at only a slightly lower price is a strong one, so pressure to
defect is great. But if players are wise, they already know the end game so they’d rather split the
market at a higher price than at a price equal to marginal cost. The fly in the ointment is that if
price is already at marginal cost, raising price is very difficult. If either firm raises price, they lose
all sales for a period—a very costly outcome. An illegal phone call to a rival is of course not an
option.

Even if collusion is reached, however, it is likely to be temporary at best in these types of markets.
Others can often enter and quickly spoil the norms. Or, if there are many firms, no firm can be
certain that all rivals understand clearly what’s going on. So they expect price cutting and reason
their way to the bottom at the outset.

An important variation on the standard Bertrand model is as follows: What if marginal costs are not
equal while goods remain homogeneous? In these circumstances the lower cost firm easily drives the
other out of the market. However, to do so, the low-cost firm need not price at their own
marginal cost; instead, they price just below the high-cost firm’s marginal cost. That way, they
maximize their profit on each unit sold while still taking the whole market.

A final variation of the model is to impose capacity constraints. A Bertrand firm can take the
whole market by undercutting their rival, but if they have capacity constraints and cannot serve
the whole market, undercutting is pointless. To do so would simply reduce profitability by
lowering price without raising sales (once capacity is filled). But capacity constraints have effects
beyond just eliminating price cuts. They actually put upward pressure on price. Firms reason that
all they have to do is fill their capacity, at any price. So they raise price up to the point that sales
just dip below their target capacity and then stop. With homogeneous firms, capacity constraints
mean that prices above marginal cost may be sustainable in Standard Bertrand.

Stackleberg

Stackleberg oligopoly is mentioned here only briefly and no formal models are provided.
Stackleberg competition refers to a very specific case of a race to build or expand capacity in a
market in which it becomes clear that one firm will make its capacity choices before others. Both
the leader firm and the follower firms know about the others. In the two-firm case, Firm 1 makes
its build choices before Firm 2, knowing that Firm 2 will also build capacity. Firm 2 knows that
Firm 1 knows this and so builds a smaller plant while Firm 1 builds a larger plant. Thus,
Stackleberg is a type of first-mover advantage in which the leader takes a greater market share
than the follower. However, the formal model is complicated and probably the most unrealistic
of all the models discussed here so let’s move on …

Differentiated Bertrand8

Differentiated Bertrand is the most common type of oligopoly in today’s world. It is a market in
which goods are moderately differentiated and price is the primary strategic variable. Think of
cola, desktop computers, automobiles, airlines, ski resorts, and a long list of other markets.

In contrast to standard Bertrand competition, high sustainable prices in differentiated Bertrand
markets are possible because of product differentiation. The reasoning is as follows: Suppose that
Firm 1 and Firm 2 compete together with products that are differentiated but are nevertheless
strong substitutes. Because Firm 1 has a loyal following among at least some of its customers, it
can command a positive (but low) price for its product even if Firm 2 is giving away its product
for nothing (many loyal Coke drinkers would still pay 10 cents for a can of Coke even if a free can
of Pepsi were being offered right beside it). In terms of Figure 3 below, even if p2 is 0, Firm 1 can
charge positive price p1min. Firm 2 enjoys the same effects with their product—they can charge a
positive price and get some sales even if Firm 1 is giving away its product for free. Since both
firms know this to be the case, each reasons that they can increase price. They each know that the
differences in product features mean that if their rival moves from 0 to a higher price, they can
charge that higher price plus a premium for some of their customers. So each firm continues to
raise price until they’ve exhausted the willingness of consumers to pay the premium. In practice,
each additional price increase lowers the premium that consumers are willing to pay until finally
consumers reach a point at which they’re indifferent between products. This happens as
elasticity changes along the demand curve. The effect is upward sloping reaction functions that
eventually cross.9

Determining Market Structure and Competition

When examining markets to ascertain the type of market structure (i.e. monopoly, oligopoly, etc.)
and competition (i.e. Cournot, Bertrand, etc.), focus on a few key market and firm characteristics.

          market.10 Rather, competition in retail gasoline mimics oligopoly at local intersections.
