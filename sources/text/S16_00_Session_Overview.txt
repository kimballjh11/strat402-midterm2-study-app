 Production Functions 

 A production function turns inputs into output, and an isoquant is every combination of capital and labor that produces the same amount. Seen in 3D, an isoquant is a slice through the production surface at one height. This workbook draws the surface and its isoquants for three common production functions. Nothing is graded and there's nothing to fill in: change the parameters and watch how the shape changes.

 Experiment 

 Linear 
 Q = aK + bL 
 Capital and labor are perfect substitutes, so the isoquants are straight lines. Change a and b and watch the slope of the lines tilt toward the more productive input. 

 Cobb-Douglas 
 Q = K a L b 
 Inputs substitute imperfectly, so the isoquants bow toward the origin. Try values of a and b that sum to less than, equal to, and more than 1, and see how the surface bends. 

 Leontief 
 Q = min{aK, bL} 
 Inputs must be used in fixed proportions, so the isoquants are L-shaped. Change a and b and watch where the corners move. 

 Changing Levels of Production 
 Q = AK a L b 
 The chart shows the isoquant for 100 units. Type a new output level into the blank Adjusted Quantity cell (G2) to draw a second isoquant, and see which way it shifts. 

 On the 3D charts, each colored band is a range of output. Look straight down on the surface and those bands become the isoquant map in the 2D chart beside it.

 Cost Minimization 

 A firm minimizes the cost of producing a given output when the last dollar spent on each input buys the same amount of output. In this exercise you'll compare the marginal product per dollar of labor and of capital for a production line, decide which input the firm should use more of, and sketch how the cost-minimizing mix shifts when the rental rate on capital rises. Download the worksheet below and work through the three questions with the people around you.

 Production Exercise 

 Background 

 In early 2024, Klarna, the buy-now-pay-later company, put an AI assistant in front of its customer service chats. Within a month Klarna said the assistant was doing the work of 700 full-time agents, and the company let its human support staff shrink through a hiring freeze. A year later the CEO said the push had gone too far: cost had driven the decision, quality had slipped, and Klarna started recruiting human agents again. 

 Klarna's question is a production question. AI assistant hours and human agent hours are both inputs to resolving customer conversations, and they substitute for each other, but not perfectly. In this assignment you will estimate Klarna's customer service production function from monthly data, trace out its marginal products and isoquant, and use input prices to test whether Klarna is using the cost-minimizing mix of AI and human hours. Then you will find that mix with Solver, measure what moving to it would save, and translate the answer into the number of agents Klarna should employ. How far to automate, and how much to trust a model that recommends it, is a strategic decision nearly every service business now faces. 

 The monthly data are simulated for this exercise and calibrated to Klarna's public statements; they are not Klarna's internal records.

 Instructions 

 Start with the Overview tab, then complete all nine steps on the Exercise tab. The Data tab holds 32 months of Klarna's conversations resolved, AI assistant hours, and human agent hours. You will need Excel's Analysis ToolPak and Solver add-ins; the Log-linear Regression and Using Solver to Cost Minimize tabs explain how to use them. 

 Estimate the production function (step 1). Take the natural log of output and both inputs, run a log-linear regression, and write out Klarna's Cobb-Douglas production function. 

 Chart production and marginal products (steps 2–3). Plot the total product of human agent hours, then the marginal products of human and AI hours, holding the other input at its monthly average. 

 Map the isoquant (step 4). Plot every mix of AI and human hours that produces Klarna's average monthly output, and mark where Klarna actually operates. 

 Find the cost-minimizing mix (steps 5–6). Compare the marginal product per dollar of each input at Klarna's current mix, then use Solver to find the mix that produces the same output at the lowest cost. 

 Show the tangency (step 7). Plot the isoquant and the isocost line on one chart so they touch at the cost-minimizing mix. 

 Measure the payoff (steps 8–9). Calculate the monthly and annual savings, and how the number of full-time human agents would change. 

 Answer the four questions. Answer in complete sentences, in terms Klarna's head of customer service could act on.
