The Quantitative Skills of a Strategist 
 
Strategic management is all about helping companies achieve superior performance. Good strategists 
will make decisions informed by rigorous analysis of data. You can learn how to analyze data and make 
good decisions by enhancing your quantitative analysis skills. The purpose of this math review is to 
ensure that you have the necessary quantitative skills to make good strategic decisions. 
 
In strategy, you use quantitative analysis to help optimize common managerial decisions. For example, a 
business strategist may be concerned about maximizing a firm’s revenue, profit, or productivity. 
Conversely, a manager might also be interested in minimizing cost, lead times, or defects. All of these 
decisions reflect the notion that there is some optimal level of inputs that help to produce a desired 
level of output.  
 
The primary mathematical tool used to optimize common managerial decisions is calculus. Calculus is 
simply the mathematical study of continuous change. In this course, you will use our knowledge of 
calculus for two primary purposes:  
1. Optimizing common managerial decisions 
2. Isolating the effects of specific variables 
 
By the end of this review, my hope is that you will have a solid understanding about why you use 
calculus in business and how it can help you make good managerial decisions. 
 
Introduction to Mathematical Functions 
 
In order to use calculus correctly, you must first understand mathematical functions. A function is a 
quantitative formula that defines the relationship between a set of inputs and outputs. For example, 
imagine you were asked to draw the relationship between revenue and price. Intuitively, you should 
know that if you charge nothing (i.e. offer your product or service for free) your revenue will be zero. 
You should also know that if you charge an obscene amount of money demand may also be zero thus 
leading to zero revenue. What that means is that the conceptual relationship between revenue and 
price should look like an upside down parabola that intersects the x-axis at 0 (where Price = 0) and some 
larger value (where an obscene price leads to zero demand). This parabola should also have a peak or 
maximum value. The price that produces that maximum amount of revenue is known as the revenue-
maximizing price. 
 
Take for instance the function 𝑅𝑅(𝑃𝑃) = 100𝑃𝑃−2𝑃𝑃2 where R is revenue and P is price. If you graph this 
function with R on the y-axis and P on the x-axis you should see something similar to Figure 1 below. As 
you can observe, this function intersects the x-axis at P = 0 and P = 50. Visually it appears that revenue is 
maximized at a P = 25.  Although the revenue-maximizing price in this figure is readily observable, that 
may not always be the case, particularly with a more complex function that is harder to graph.

An Application of Calculus in Business: The Revenue-Maximizing Price 
 
Above I introduced the function 𝑅𝑅(𝑃𝑃) = 100𝑃𝑃−2𝑃𝑃2 where R is revenue and P is price. If I were to ask 
you to determine the revenue maximizing price, you could do it in one of three ways: 
 
1. You could plug various prices into the function to see if revenue increases or decreases, 
ultimately zeroing on the revenue maximizing price using trial-and-error. While simple, this 
process is likely to be inefficient, particularly for complex polynomial functions with multiple 
terms. 
2. You could graph the function and visually estimate the revenue-maximizing price. Again, this 
could readily be accomplished with simple functions, but could be extremely hard to do with 
more complex functions. 
3. You could use calculus to solve for the revenue-maximizing price. This three-step process relies 
upon the notion that you know something unique about the maximal point of a function i.e. that 
the instantaneous rate of change or the slope of the function at any maximum point is equal to 
zero. As you will see, this process is likely to be the most efficient method for determining the 
revenue-maximizing price because you can solve for the optimal price in three simple steps.

Isolating Effects of Individual Variables 
 
Sometimes the output or dependent variable that you care about is affected by more than one input i.e. 
the outcome is a multivariate function of several independent variables. For example, suppose you’re a 
product manager at Apple and you’re trying to forecast how many IPhones you might sell next quarter. 
What other factors besides the price might influence how many IPhones you sell?  
 
Certainly, the price of substitute goods (i.e. Android phones) could have an impact. What about the 
overall health of the economy? Would advertising make a difference? Change in consumer tastes? The 
list could go on and on. The bottom line is that many outcomes managers care about, such as demand, 
are impacted by multiple independent variables. Such situations, while very common, make managing 
an outcome like demand more complex. How for example, would demand change if you raised the 
price, knowing that demand can also be affected by many other factors including the price of substitute 
goods, advertising, income of the target market, etc. To solve this conundrum, you would use calculus! 
 
Let’s continue on with IPhone example. Suppose that the forecasted demand for the IPhone next 
quarter can be represented by the following function: 
 
𝑄𝑄𝐼𝐼𝐼𝐼= 1,000 −4𝑃𝑃𝐼𝐼𝐼𝐼+ 2𝑃𝑃𝑆𝑆𝑆𝑆+ 100𝐴𝐴𝐼𝐼𝐼𝐼+ 500𝐼𝐼2 
 
where QIP is quantity demanded for the IPhone, PIP is the price of the IPhone, PSG is the price of the 
Samsung Galaxy, AIP is the amount of money that Apple spends on advertising, and I is the average 
income of the target market. If Apple were to increase the price of the IPhone by just $1 and everything 
was held constant (i.e. nothing else changed), how would the forecasted demand change?
